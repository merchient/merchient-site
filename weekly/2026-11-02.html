What Meta’s ad rules catch: week of 2 November 2026

Every line below was in a supplement ad running on Meta when we read it. Each one meets a rule that Meta’s health-claims review, the FTC or the FDA acts on.

The week in numbers

We read 170 supplement ads on 30 and 31 October and 1 November, one from each of 170 brands.

  • Red: 64. Wording a regulator or a platform acts on.
  • Yellow: 58. Every other finding, less likely to be acted on.
  • Green: 48. Nothing to flag.

The three rules met most often, across the Red and Yellow lines:

  1. FDA: a line that promises relief from a disease or a symptom. 31 ads.
  2. FTC: proof words such as “clinically proven”, “the only” or “#1”. 27 ads.
  3. Meta: a line that tells or asks the viewer about their own condition. 19 ads.

Of the 122 Red and Yellow lines, 54 met an FDA rule, 37 an FTC rule and 31 a Meta rule.

Eight lines, all Red

Quotes are shortened. Brand names, product names and ad identifiers are left out.

Red

1. Easing a symptom

The line“…can help ease joint pain and stiffness”
RuleFDA, disease or symptom claims.
WhyEasing joint pain is relief from a symptom, which the FDA reads as a drug claim a supplement can’t make.
Safer“Helps support healthy joints and flexibility.”

Red

2. Fixing a number tied to a disease

The line“Your blood sugar will be balanced…”
RuleFDA, disease or symptom claims.
WhyA promise to fix a body level tied to a disease, here blood sugar, reads as a disease claim to the FDA.
Safer“Helps maintain blood sugar levels already within the normal range.”

Red

3. Next to a prescription drug

The linean ad for a prostate supplement that tells the viewer no prescription drug is hidden in it.
RuleFDA, comparison with a prescription drug. Meta’s health-claims review flags it too.
WhyThe line sets the product beside a prescription drug, and the FDA reads that kind of comparison as a disease claim.
Safer“A daily supplement to support healthy prostate function.”

Red

4. Pitched to people on a drug

The linea headline offering support to “GLP-1 users”.
RuleFDA, comparison with a prescription drug. Meta’s health-claims review flags it too.
WhyPitching a supplement to people taking a prescription drug sets it beside that drug, and the FDA reads that kind of comparison as a disease claim.
Safer“Essential Daily Nutritional Support”

Red

5. A question about the viewer’s body

The linea question that asks the viewer about several digestive symptoms at once.
RuleMeta, the personal-attributes rule.
WhyThe line asks the viewer about their own body, and Meta’s rule bars an ad that tells or asks the viewer about their own condition.
Safer“Looking for everyday support for digestion and regularity?”

Red

6. A weight-loss goal

The line“…boost your metabolism and support your weight loss goals”
RuleMeta, health-and-wellness standards.
WhyMeta’s standards reject weight-loss promises, and this line ties the product to the buyer’s weight-loss goals.
Safer“A delicious superfruit drink to support a healthy metabolism.”

Red

7. A joke about a body part

The linea before-and-after joke about the shape of a body part, ahead of summer.
RuleMeta, health-and-wellness standards.
WhyThe joke makes a body part the problem to fix, and Meta’s standards bar an ad that picks on a body part or appearance.
Safer“Ready to feel great in your favorite summer looks?”

Red

8. A result with a deadline

The line“Natural Relief in Weeks”
RuleFTC, a result by a deadline.
Why“In weeks” puts a time on the result, and the FTC reads a deadline as a promise the brand has to prove for a typical customer.
Safer“Natural support for everyday comfort.”
