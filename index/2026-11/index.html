Supplement ad index: November 2026

Supplement ads running on Meta, the share that carried a line Vera flags, the rules most hit and how long the ads had been running when we read them.

Ads read

From 30 October 2026 to 2 November 2026 we read 262 supplement ads in Meta’s public Ad Library, in five sweeps, one ad from each of 262 brands (counted by the website the ad leads to). Vera, Merchient’s AI employee, read each ad’s words against Meta’s ad rules and the FTC and FDA guidance, and gave it a tier.

  • Red. Wording Vera expects a regulator or a platform to act on.
  • Yellow. Every other finding, less likely to be acted on.
  • Green. Nothing to flag.

194 of the 262 ads (74%) carried a line Vera flags, Red or Yellow.

The five rules most hit

Each flagged ad is counted once, under the rule its reason points to; we sorted Vera’s reasons into these groups. These five cover 163 of the 194 flagged ads. The other 31 are spread over six smaller groups; 8 of those were flagged by a word check that named no rule. Examples are described, not quoted: no brand, product name or ad is named.

  1. FDA: a line that promises relief from, or names, a disease or a symptom. 63 ads, 57 of them Red. The FDA reads a line that says a product relieves, fixes or prevents a disease or a symptom as a drug claim. A supplement can describe how it supports the body; it can’t say it treats a condition. Example: A headline that promises to fix a named gland.
  2. FTC: proof or ranking words with no basis shown. 41 ads, none of them Red. The FTC expects a brand to be able to prove what its words claim. “Clinically proven”, “science-backed”, “best-selling” and “the only” need evidence behind them, and an ad that shows none is flagged. Example: An ad that calls its product “scientifically proven” and names no study.
  3. Meta: a line that tells or asks the viewer about their own health. 29 ads, 20 of them Red. Meta’s personal-attributes rule bars an ad that tells or asks the viewer about their own health, with words such as “you have”, “struggling with” or “diagnosed with”. Example: A line that tells the viewer they have a named urinary symptom.
  4. FDA: the product set beside a prescription drug, a medical device or surgery. 18 ads, 17 of them Red. Naming a prescription drug, a medical device or surgery next to a supplement, or pitching the supplement to people on a drug, reads to the FDA as a disease claim, and Meta’s health-claims review flags it too. Example: A headline that offers a supplement to people on a weight-loss drug.
  5. Meta: a weight-loss promise, or picking on a body part. 12 ads, 10 of them Red. Meta’s health-and-wellness standards bar weight-loss promises and before-and-after bodies, and an ad that picks on a body part or appearance. Example: A review that sells a product by the slimmer waist it gave.

By category

Ads are grouped by the search word that found them, one group for each ad. “Other” holds the searches that fit none of the groups above it, such as prostate, blood pressure, ashwagandha and shilajit.

  • GLP-1 and weight. 31 ads read, 23 flagged (74%). Most hit, tied at 6 ads each: beside a prescription drug (FDA) and proof or ranking words (FTC).
  • Sleep. 8 ads read, 5 flagged (63%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 3 ads.
  • Gut. 33 ads read, 27 flagged (82%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 10 ads.
  • Immunity. 11 ads read, 9 flagged (82%). Most hit: proof or ranking words (FTC), 5 ads.
  • Brain. 20 ads read, 11 flagged (55%). Most hit, tied at 3 ads each: disease or symptom claim (FDA) and proof or ranking words (FTC).
  • Hormones. 53 ads read, 41 flagged (77%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 18 ads.
  • Joints. 12 ads read, 12 flagged (100%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 8 ads.
  • Skin and hair. 12 ads read, 10 flagged (83%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 4 ads.
  • Other. 82 ads read, 56 flagged (68%). Most hit: disease or symptom claim (FDA), 14 ads.

Small groups: Sleep, Immunity, Joints and Skin and hair each have fewer than 15 ads, so read their shares as counts.

What it shows. Most of these ads were not new. In every tier more than half (56% to 63%) had been running for 90 days or longer when we read them. The median Red ad had been running 103 days, the median Green ad 128.

What it does not show. These ads were running when we read them, so this cannot say how many flagged ads were stopped, by the brand or by Meta: an ad that had stopped before a sweep is not in these numbers. A recheck of the same ads would show that; this issue does not. It does not say whether Meta has reviewed any of these ads or what it decided. Four of the five sweeps took the library’s active ads sorted by impressions, so the ads lean towards those that reach many people; the first sweep did not record its order, or its filters. These ads are not a random sample of supplement ads.

What changed since last issue

This is the first issue, so there is nothing to compare with yet.

Method

We read supplement ads in Meta’s public Ad Library: active ads for the United States (the first sweep did not record its filters), found by search words such as the ones grouped above, one ad from each brand. Vera, an AI, read each ad’s words against Meta’s published ad rules and the FTC and FDA guidance. A tier and a rule show what Vera flagged, never what Meta rejected. We do not know what Meta decided about any of these ads. Vera read each ad’s headline and the start of its text, not its picture or video or the page it leads to, so a Green ad had nothing to flag in those words and may still have a problem elsewhere. Start dates are the ones the library shows.
